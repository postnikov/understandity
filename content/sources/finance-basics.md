These are fictional teaching models, not financial advice. Amounts are tokens; rates stay fixed; ignore taxes and fees. Separate the balance from growth, purchasing power, obligations, risk, and access.

1. Compound interest. Start with 100 tokens earning 10 percent yearly. Interest stays: after one year, 110; after two, 121, because the second year's interest includes interest on earlier interest. Change only whether interest earns interest: simple interest gives 120. Transfer: a club's 200 tokens under the compound rule become 242 after two years, without deposits or withdrawals.

2. Inflation. A general rise in prices reduces purchasing power. Cash under a mattress keeps its face value while buying less. A basket rises from 100 to 110; unchanged cash no longer buys it. Change only the basket's price to 100. Transfer: savings rise from 100 to 105 while the same basket reaches 110; the larger balance buys less than one basket. Individual prices need not rise equally.

3. Borrowing versus saving. Debt interest increases obligations; deposit interest increases savings. With no interim payments, borrowing 100 for one year at 20 percent and depositing it at 5 percent leaves 105 owned against 120 owed: a 15-token shortfall. Change only the debt rate to 5 percent. Transfer: borrowing 200 at 10 percent to save at 5 percent leaves a 10-token shortfall. Credit cards may calculate interest daily; payment and grace-period rules matter.

4. Diversification. Different names do not guarantee different risks. Two fictional businesses depending on one bridge can both lose revenue when it closes. Change only one business's dependence on that bridge. Transfer: several investments in ski resorts remain exposed to one snowless winter. Spreading exposure can reduce concentration risk, never guarantee no losses.

5. Emergency reserves. Availability matters as well as size. A fictional locked account holds 120 tokens, inaccessible until next month; an unexpected 80-token repair is due today. Change only the release date to today. Transfer: a school club owns valuable equipment but cannot sell it before tomorrow's bill. Wealth is not necessarily spendable cash.
